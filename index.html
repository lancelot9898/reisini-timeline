Nicolas Reisini Nikolai Reizini · Nicolas G. Reisini · Наум Георгиевич Рейзин · “N. Reisin” · “Boruh Resnikoff”

1905 to 1981  ·  Salonika (disputed) → Paris → Harbin → Shanghai → New York

In Paris he was a café philosopher who started a Russian literary journal. Memoirs and historians say France expelled him. By his account he ran a coal mine in Manchuria. In Shanghai he ran a film company, and the police there suspected him of spying, though it was never confirmed.

He came to America on a Greek diplomatic passport. Later he was shut out of the country at the Mexican border for sixteen months. In those same years he was running Cinerama shows abroad. They were seen as propaganda victories for the United States, so the U.S. Information Agency took an interest in his case. Then he took control of Cinerama and announced the Cinerama Dome on Sunset Boulevard. The loan that paid for Cinerama’s expansion later cost him the company.

The four versions of who he was do not agree

Each version below comes from a written record. No birth record has ever been found to show which one is right. His fight to enter the United States in 1955 and 1956 turned partly on which version was true. In November 1956 an appeals board found him truthful about where he was born and let him in.

His own account

Born in Salonika, Greece, 1905. Parents killed in the “Asia Minor Salonika uprisings.” Taken abroad at age two by a grandfather who was a European financier and industrialist and who paid for his schooling in Paris and Danzig. Roman Catholic. Greek national.

NYT, 8 Apr 1962 · sworn testimony, INS hearing, Sept 1955

U.S. Immigration Service, 1955

Not Greek at all, but a Russian Jew born in Russia or in Harbin. Real name possibly Nicholas Reisin or Baruch Resnikoff. The Service produced an elderly New York couple, Mr. and Mrs. Gregory Reisin, and suggested they were his parents. Both swore under oath they were not.

NYT, 19 Sept 1955 & 4 Feb 1956

Shanghai Municipal Police, file D-7992

Registration card: born 7 July 1905, “Saloniki, Greece.” Internal reports, 1937-38: “born in Harbin of Russian Jewish parents,” traveling on a Greek passport “said to have been issued in the free city of Danzig.” A 1945 report: originally “Resnikoff or Resnikov… later for a period he had Greek passport and changed his name to Reisini.”

SMP Special Branch file D-7992 / 3358 · U.S. National Archives RG 263, M1750

Russian émigré scholarship

Наум Георгиевич Рейзин (Naum Georgievich Reizin), 1905-1981, “writer and publisher of the Russian emigration, entrepreneur.” One biography of Gaito Gazdanov says he was born in Greece to Russian immigrant parents who had moved there in the early 1900s, and came to Paris in 1920.

Russian Wikipedia (Рейзин) · V. Khazan, 2024 · O. Korostelev, 2010

Chapter 1 · Origins · 1905 to 1919

A birth no one has verified

Four versions of where he was born and who his parents were, and the childhood he later described to The New York Times and under oath.

7 July 1905

Birth, on paper

Shanghai Municipal Police registration card No. 776, filed inside Special Branch file D-7992, records his birth as 7 July 1905 at “Saloniki, Greece.” Russian émigré reference works give 1905 as well; one scholarly entry hedges with “1902? 1905?” The 1950 U.S. census taker wrote his age as 44; the judge who sentenced him in August 1979 was told he was 74. Both are consistent with 1905.

1905, the other versions

“Really of Russian parentage”

The U.S. Immigration Service argued at his 1955 hearing that he was born not in Greece but in Russia or Harbin, and that he was a Russian Jew rather than a Greek Catholic. It also argued that his real name might be Nicholas Reisin or Baruch Resnikoff. Shanghai police reports of 1937-38 independently described him as “born in Harbin of Russian Jewish parents.” A 1945 SMP report typed it plainly: “Resnikoff or Resnikov, typical Jewish name. Later for a period he had Greek passport and changed his name to Reisini.”

Chapter 2 · Paris · 1920 to 1934

A café philosopher in Russian Paris

He sits in on the philosopher Lev Shestov’s lectures at the Sorbonne, becomes a regular in the Montparnasse cafés and starts the journal Chisla (Numbers). The writer Vasily Yanovsky, who knew him, wrote that he was then expelled from France, and later historians say the same.

1920

Arrives in Paris and “begins to engage in literature”

The Gazdanov biography dates his arrival precisely: “In 1920, Nikolai came to Paris and began to engage in literature.” He was fifteen. Nothing between the alleged Salonika birth and this line has been documented by anyone.

1929 to 1930

He invents «Числа»

He meets Irma de Manziarly, a theosophist close to Krishnamurti, and talks her into funding a Russian journal. Then he negotiates with the influential figures of Montparnasse. 1933 to 1934

The Hachette affair, and expulsion from France

According to Yanovsky, there were irregularities in the accounts at Hachette: missing deluxe illustrated volumes, which Reizini had been handing out to drinking companions on Montparnasse after midnight. (“He practically forced Felzen to take home an encyclopedia of parrots in colour.”) He was prosecuted, Yanovsky writes, and as a foreigner “and, one might say, an undesirable one,” expulsion was inevitable. Money was collected for him, he writes, at the Numbers office near the Convention métro. Other sources say simply that the authorities suspected him of financial fraud and expelled him.

Chapter 3 · The Far East · 1934 to 1946

Ten years in a Shanghai police file

He told The New York Times he ran a coal mine in Manchuria. The police file shows a film company in Shanghai and ten years of suspicion of spying and smuggling, none of it confirmed.

1933, his version

“China’s second largest coal mine”

He told the Times that in 1933, aged 28, he went to Manchuria to run a coal mine his grandfather had given him. He said he turned a failing pit into China’s second-largest coal mine and became the Chinese distributor of European films such as “Mayerling” with Charles Boyer. Variety heard the same story in 1961: “In the early ’30s he inherited from his grandfather some coal mines in Manchuria.”

22 February 1945

The last police report, and the sharpest

He arrives from Tientsin and takes room 452 at the Cathay Hotel. The Japanese-controlled Foreign Affairs section writes him up. It describes him as connected with the Asia Industrial & Mercantile Company and with the Manchukuo Economic Mission, with the “reputation of a successful businessman who has several influential Japanese and Chinese friends who are backing him as he assists them in business transactions, some of them of illegal character”. It also reports that he is engaged in smuggling between Manchukuo, North China and Shanghai with wealthy Jewish businessmen “a number of whom are closely connected with Soviet circles.” His visitors include a representative of a “camouflaged Soviet firm” financed by the Moscow Narodny Bank, and Henry (Genady) Levitin, a Soviet citizen “suspected of being connected with Soviet Intelligence.” He is rumored to be carrying a letter from Dr Abraham Kaufman, head of Manchuria’s Jewish community, to the Jews of Shanghai. The Anaconda scheme

Charles H. Kraft is treasurer of the Anaconda Company. Over seven years he falsely assures a succession of banks that companies controlled by Reisini and his associate James L. Open questions

No document I have found answers these ten questions. Your family’s memories and papers are the best place left to look. If you know an answer, or know someone who might, please tell me.

Where and when did he die?

Only the year, 1981, and only from Russian reference works. No obituary, no death certificate, no burial. This is the single largest hole and probably the easiest for the family to close.

Who was his wife (or wives)?

Shanghai police records place a wife with him in January 1937 and on the boat out in September 1937. American trade press in 1962 says he met a Hungarian woman in New York, married her in Mexico, and had the first two of three children in London. Same woman, or two marriages?

Where was he really born?

Salonika 1905 (his account and his SMP card), Harbin or Russia (Immigration Service and 1937-38 police reports), or Greece to Russian parents (the Gazdanov biography). A Greek birth record, a Harbin registry, or a family document would settle decades of argument.

What happened in Paris on 25 October 1931?

An alleged arrest for “breach of trust”, one of the two grounds on which he was kept out of the country for sixteen months in 1955 and 1956. The French case file has never been located, and in 1936 the Shanghai police recorded that the French police “failed to disclose any trace” of his stay in Paris.

Did he ever become an American citizen?

Senator Humphrey said in October 1962 that he was “about to become” one. The 1950 census says he was not naturalized. No naturalization record has surfaced.

What is in the 16 July 1947 Army intelligence memorandum?

A U.S. Army memo with his name as its subject is cited in a State Department file, but the memo itself has not been located. It would be the American counterpart to the Shanghai police file.

Did the film-exchange offer to Moscow ever go through?

He proposed swapping Cinerama for Kinopanorama in March 1958, and Kinopanorama played New York in June 1959. But no document yet ties his company to that booking, or to “Cinerama’s Russian Adventure” (1966), which came after his ouster.

What became of the Mariel shipyard?

An $86 million Cuban deal announced eight weeks before Batista fled. Whether a dollar was ever spent, and what happened to the Cuban bank subscription, is unrecorded in anything found so far.

Who were the Reisins of New York?

The elderly couple the Immigration Service produced in 1955 and suggested were his parents. They denied it under oath. Nobody has ever established who they were, or why the Service believed it.

Where did the papers go?

Compare the case of David Baerwald. He found two boxes of his grandfather’s diaries, letters and a spy camera in a house threatened by wildfire, and got a 600-page novel out of them. If anything comparable survives here (passports, the Greek diplomatic papers, Shanghai correspondence, photographs), it could answer many of these questions.
